ABOUT THIS DEMONSTRATION

A simple model.
A visible chain of cause and effect.

The 3D layouts, replacement values, and component depth–damage curves are invented for teaching. They are not fitted or reproduced results from the cited paper.

What comes from the study?
Archetype F1 is a one-story home on a crawlspace; F3 has two stories on a crawlspace; F12 is a hospital/clinic. The houses use the study’s 1 m (3.28 ft) first-floor elevation. The clinic layout is a simplified one-story interpretation.
What does the water control mean?
Peak flood depth above ground. The second readout shows depth relative to the first floor. Indoor and outdoor water levels are assumed equal. Lowering the slider compares a smaller hypothetical flood; it does not repair damage from a previous event.
How are losses calculated?
Each component has a value and a piecewise linear depth–damage function relative to its floor. Loss is value × damage fraction. Category curves sum those losses, divided by each category’s own total value. Framing, roof, foundation, finishes, systems and contents are counted once. Land and indirect losses are excluded.
What does red mean?
More red means a larger modeled damage fraction. Red on an assembly represents repair loss, not a prediction of collapse or loss of structural safety. Foundations have small assumed repair fractions; sensitive contents can reach full replacement.
What is simplified?
There is no flood duration, velocity, contamination, water pressure, drying, or hydraulic simulation. The two-story model assumes a 3.2 m floor-to-floor height. Elevating an item shifts its damage curve up by 2 ft, without modeling installation costs or feasibility.
Why does 100% sometimes say “replace”?
In this demonstration, full loss of an individual item means its assumed replacement cost. Intermediate damage is an illustrative repair/replacement fraction, not a probability of failure.
